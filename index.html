Data: What Are We Sending?

Pictures, music, text, videos, games, and websites look different to us. Inside a computer, they are all represented as data.

Computers represent digital information using binary: patterns of 0s and 1s. Programs interpret that data to show us pictures, play music, and display webpages.

Click the switches to change the pattern. Each is one bit, either 0 or 1.

Packets: Breaking It Down

Even a short sentence can take lots of 0s and 1s to represent. Sending everything as one giant chunk can cause problems: if something goes wrong, a lot might need to be sent again, and big transmissions occupy network resources.

Solution: Break information into smaller pieces called packets, send the pieces through the network, and put the information back together when it arrives.

Routing: Finding the Way

Packets have a destination, but they don't carry a complete map of the route ahead. Routers use information about network paths to decide which connection to forward a packet along next.

Think of a road trip: You know where you want to go, but ask for directions at each gas station. Different trips might take different roads and arrive at different times.

Packet Anatomy: What's Inside?

A real packet can contain a lot of technical information. You do not need to memorize every field. In our simplified classroom model, focus on four concepts:

FROMComputer A
TOComputer B
ORDER3 of 4
DATABA

FROM = sender • TO = destination • ORDER = where it belongs • DATA = message content

Real network protocols spread these jobs across different headers and layers. Packet ordering in particular may be handled by TCP. The four boxes above are a teaching model.

Paper Activity: You Are the Router

Complete this part on the paper worksheet your teacher hands out.

Mission: Send MEATBALL from Computer A to Computer B using four numbered packets.

  1. Split MEATBALL into four packets.
  2. Choose a different route for each packet on the worksheet's network map.
  3. Record the routers visited on each route.
  4. Add the connection costs to find each route's total.
  5. Put the packets back together at their destination.
Real packets don't have to take different routes. That's a special rule for our classroom challenge.
